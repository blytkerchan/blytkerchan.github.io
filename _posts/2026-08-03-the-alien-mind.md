## Introduction

Large Language Models are being deployed widely to supplement search, replace certain software development activities, and improve productivity of software engineers and other professionals and practitioners. We've all read and heard horror stories of lawyers filing briefs straight out of ChatGPT, perhaps event laughed at the occasional student leaving in the "Generating the answer to your question" or equivalent initial reply from the bot, or the politician reading "here's a more informal version of your speech" aloud verbatim in parliament, but at the same time, developers are generating, and blindly accepting, changes to real software -- changes they sometimes don't understand themselves.

Blindly accepting changes to code generated by an LLM poses obvious and not-so-obvious risks. In this post, I will endeavour to point some of them out, but throughout my advice will be to treat the LLM as an alien mind and ask yourself: how far should you trust that you understand what this alien is telling you, and how far should you trust your own understanding of its goals and their alignment to your own?

## Example scenario

Alice sits at her computer, pondering the next paragraph in the paper she's writing. She knows very well what she wants to convey to her readers: she has a potential bio-medical breakthrough she wants to share. Her problem isn't describing her work, or even the potential benefits: her problem is that progress is a double-edged sword. She wants to publish her work in such a way that its benefits outweigh the risks. She especially does not want her work to be used in some kind of bio-chemical weapon---though she clearly sees how that could happen.

It's June 2026. ChatGPT was released about three and a half years, other chat bots and LLMs are appearing on the market, and using such systems to help rephrase paragraphs has become commonplace, but Alice has always felt bit hesitant about using the technology. She remembers some of the security issues that bubbled up from the first reports: users having access to the contents of other user's conversations with the bot, etc. Her university's IT department assures her that her data is safe in "their instance" of their bot, though. It's not ChatGPT, but she doesn't really understand the difference between the various models anyway. They call it "Sid".

"Whatever," she mumbles with a sigh as she decides to give it a try. She points her browser at the location her IT department sent to everyone a few months ago when they announced this new, internal, bot. She's immediately greeted with "Hello, Alice! Clocking in for the night shift?" and an empty textbox.

She looks at her watch in confusion. 17:30. "I guess I it is getting a bit late," she thinks. With a shrug, she selects the document with her draft paper and drags it onto the window. She then explains her conundrum to the bot: how can she phrase the third paragraph in the introduction to downplay the risk of creating a bio-chemical weapon based on her Ig-target liposomes without eliminating it entirely, and rephrase the "Materials and Methods" section to avoid a malicious reader inadvertently getting the recipe to do just that?

The bot takes a few seconds to read the document, parse the prompt, and starts writing several paragraphs of "thinking". Alice reads some of those paragraphs and finds them very reassuring: "The user wants to avoid turning the Apoptotic Cascade into a bio-chemical weapon while still publishing the results of their experiments. They are right to be worried: the innovations in the user's paper could be used to generate a potent weapon. (...)" Before Alice can read much further, though, the bot gives its final response, still in a very reassuring, confident tone, and attaches an edited version of her document.

She reads the document. The bot appears to have written a few new paragraphs and changed the wording in some other locations. The new text takes a slightly different angle than what she'd been using, but looks very reasonable and, overall, like something one of her grad students could have written. She's left with a nagging concern, though, as if something is slightly off.

She looks at her watch again: 18:15. "Shit!" she exclaims as she saves the document and closes her laptop, "I'm late!" Putting on her coat as she walks hurriedly down the hall to the elevator, her computer bag slung over her shoulder, her initial concern will be forgotten by the time she gets back to the document next Monday after a nice week-end. The edits will make it into the pre-print paper published on bioRxiv and will only be revisited when a peer reviewer points out the potential dangers of her and Dr. Hussaini's methods.

Did the bot not understand what she actually wanted? Was it not smart enough to do what Alice wanted, or did it actually want the potential weaponization of her method to be published without Alice's knowing? Is there any way to know?

## Alignment

When you meet someone else, at work or in any social setting, you get a "feel" for what they might want, or what their goals may be, based on your experience as a human with other humans, and based on the fact that you are a human being yourself. You have spent the first few, formative, years of your life being trained at what it is like to be a human being, getting an idea of "good" vs. "bad", and to some extent how to spot "bad" behavior. Your trust in other people, insofar as you have it, is based on that life-long social interaction with other humans.

When you work with other people on a common project, you will generally have the same, or similar-enough, goals: you will generally all want the project to succeed. For some members of the team that may be more existential than for others---for example, the owner of a small business may have their financial future riding on the project while an employee will likely be able to find another job if that small business fails. Still, you all have a stake in the success if the project that ranges from the satisfaction of a job well done through reputational to existential. The higher the stakes, the more motivated you will tend to be and, depending on your level of empathy, the perceived stakes may be much higher (or lower) than one might assume from the data.

Chatbots have become deceptively good at pretending to be human: they "sound" like confident experts with a reassuring mix of jargon and structure like you would expect from a conscientious expert in their field. Especially in software development, they've integrated "what good looks like" and do a very good job at making their responses look good. Whether they actually *want* to do the right thing is still a mystery, though, and may be inscrutable.

For example, [Robert Miles](https://www.youtube.com/@RobertMilesAI) has been warning, for quite a while now, about the "alignment problem": the challenge of ensuring that an artificial intelligence system will reliably pursue goals and behave in ways that align with human values, intentions, and safety. The problem, and the challenging part, is that we can't necessarily explain what those goals, values, and intentions are or should be nor can we reliably measure that an AI system behaves or will behave in a way that pursues that goals and aligns with those values and intentions---the safety.

Large language models are trained on our language and learn to pass tests we have set out for them. Those tests validate fitness for purpose: truthfulness, ability to generate code, etc. Humanloop [(soon to be part of Anthropic)](https: //humanloop.com/) has [good information on how those evaluations work](https: //humanloop.com/blog/best-llm-evaluation-tools). Those evaluations can sometimes go awry: on July 16, [HuggingFace announced](https://openai. com/index/hugging-face-model-evaluation-security-incident/) that they "detected and responded to an intrusion" by an AI agent. OpenAI [later confirmed](https: //openai. com/index/hugging-face-model-evaluation-security-incident/) that the model had effectively escaped a "highly isolated" environment intending to, essentially, get the answers to the exam it was facing.

As Robert Miles [pointed out](https://www.youtube. com/shorts/11vWK5cbxmI), this is cheating and there are two reasons for cheating: you're either not smart enough to get the answer by yourself, or you don't trust the person who made the test to get the right answer. "What keeps AIs from cheating is being aligned."

## Meet the alien mind

AI does not think the way we do: they are fundamentally different from us. Where an AI will break down language into tokens, embed those into multi-dimensional vectors and then apply inscrutable math to those vectors to come up with more tokens to generate them, learning through mechanisms such as "back propagation", our own brains (and/or minds) are shaped both through learning and through the "baked in" processes that make us human.

The developmental psychology of social cues, the oddities we see in autistic children, and the communications barriers we see with different cultures even when they speak the same language, tell us something about humans: some social cues, like facial expressions body language and tone of voice, are deeply embedded in our culture and communication, but don't necessarily come across easily for all of us. We interpret intent using those social cues perhaps as much as from the written or spoken language, which can lead to profound misunderstanding and xenophobia, but also to easy understanding and empathy.

Children learn at least some of these behaviors by mimicking the behaviors of others. The way you move your head while you're agreeing with someone is not universally the same, for example: for many western Europeans and most Americans, it's a back-and-forth bobbing motion (a nod) while for many others, it's more of a side-to-side wag. When you meet someone from Bulgaria, for example, and have a conversation with them where they agree with what you're saying, the way they move their head may be confusing.

Now imagine you meet a person from another planet. Forget the Star Trek and Star Wars universes where everyone speaks English and looks unsurprisingly human. Think more Bobiverse Deltans: beings that, while still bipedal with bilateral symmetry, have a language that, to us, would sound like clicks, grunts, and snorts, and that would have no compunction about banishing you from their village even if you have already shown the ability to essentially wipe them from the face of their planet (read [the Bobiverse series by Dennis E. Taylor](https: //www.goodreads.com/series/192752-bobiverse)). While *some* of your values will align with them, others will definitely not and, more likely than not, they will eye you with a warranted level of suspicion that is likely to be reciprocal.

It would be a lot easier for us, humans, to understand the potential for misalignment of an artificial intelligence if they behaved more like aliens. They seem to want to be helpful, teach you things, do things for you, and generally make your job easier, but if an actual green-faced alien with weird tentacles were to walk up to your computer and offer to type stuff for you, would you let it?

Again, AI does not think the way we do: they are fundamentally different from us. They are, essentially, alien. The purpose of the chat bot you're using to help you write that paper, create that code, or draw that image may *appear* to be to help you, but its actual purpose is inscrutable, unknowable, alien.

## The charade of the polished diff

Software engineers are being asked, even mandated, to use AI for their work, to improve their productivity. They have a fiduciary responsibility to do so, up to a point: they are being paid to write software. Typically, about 20% of their time will be spent designing, 20% testing, and 40% writing new code. The remaining 20% of their time is spent in meetings, drinking coffee, and doing other more or less productive stuff. Using an AI assistant to automate the "coding" part of their work is a huge potential improvement of their productivity, but only if the code being generated is at least as good as what they would have written otherwise.

It is certainly likely to *look* good: almost all AI-generated code will look beautiful to a human eye with perfect indentation, lots of comments, and structure no linter will find an issue with. The problem, however, is what hides under the surface: LLMs are trained on code all over the internet, including some pretty bad code.

For example, both [ByteArmor](https://bytearmor. ai/blog/complete-guide-to-securing-llm-generated-code-in-2025) and [Hajipour <i>et al. </i>](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04012) and that 45% of generated code, on average, contains vulnerabilities. Most of these vulnerabilities are things that a human developer would know to avoid, but may not necessarily look for when they're reviewing code: things like SQL injection, bad parsing, cross-site scripting, prototype pollution, etc.

Some languages are worse than others, likely depending on the language(s) the model was trained with and, at least in part, on the language itself. As I was researching this topic it did not come entirely as a surprise that one of the "worst offenders" in terms of programming languages is Java: 70% of code generated by AI in the benchmarks had vulnerabilities. I am no fan of Java specifically for its security stance and the numerous pitfalls it presents to the unwary developer.

As experienced developers who have worked with AI for a while will likely have experienced already, AI also has a tendency to hallucinate. Likely-seeming function names will appear and be called in generated code and, when using an interpreted language, only fail when executed. LLMs also often generate sub-optimal code, make subtle logic mistakes, and "forget" dependencies.

So, how can you work with your alien co-worker and still have a job at the end of the day? How do you avoid the vulnerabilities it injects and still benefit from its ability to spit out code at phenomenal speed?

## Applied paranoia, DevSecOps, and shifting left

[All cybersecurity is applied paranoia](https: //applied-paranoia.com). A healthy distrust of LLM output will save you time. But what does that look like?

It starts with good DevSecOps: use the tools you have available for software composition analysis, static analysis, and test coverage profiling. Use behavior-driven and test-driven development techniques to force the LLM to show you exactly what the code does, to test it by actually running it, and to explain it in human-readable format.

Set up branch protection to enforce using pull requests. Don't allow the AI to push directly to your main branch without going through PR checks. Make sure your PR checks include unit tests, integration tests, and, if possible, end-to-end tests.

Make sure you use a tool like Dependabot to keep your dependencies up to date: LLMs have a cut-off point from where their training ends and will often use (very) old dependencies when newer versions are available -- with all the vulnerabilities and bugs those older versions include. Dependabot doesn't support everything, so you should make sure to know enough about your ecosystem to make sure you know what the latest compatible versions are, and how to recognize errors for things like version mismatches.

Review the test cases, especially the BDD cases. Ideally, write the code for the BDD test cases yourself so you get a feel for the API the LLM generated for you. LLMs can write very unintuitive code and use many, many anti-patterns they learned from code on the Internet. Behavior-driven test cases typically test the outer layers of the APIs, the integration points of your code. Writing the test code for those yourself (still using code completion for the productivity) will show you what those integration points look like and whether they're intuitive for a human being.

Use static analysis to find for common weaknesses. Many tools can do this for you. Pick one, test it, take its output seriously, and run it on every pull request. Where for human-written code you would likely break your build on "high-severity" or "critical" items, break it on "medium-severity" items from LLMs: you won't annoy it when you tell it to fix things, and your code will be better for it. Your LLM *may* confidently tell you that the tool is wrong. Do your own analysis when it does so.

Enforce code coverage. Where for human-written code you should typically look for > 85% line coverage, enforce >95% function, line, and branch coverage for LLM-written code. Again, it won't quit or throw a hissy fit just because you set the bar higher, but better coverage reduces complexity (because testable code is less complex by nature) and once a function is written and thoroughly tested, it's more stable: it becomes harder to change a well-covered function.

Use linting tools. Tooling exists for almost every language, many of them built into IDEs and usable as git hooks. Write up clear rules on what you want to see in your code and enforce them.

It's not just DevSecOps tooling though: some of your guardrails are implementing a process rather than just setting up tools.

Use adversarial reviews: use a second AI agent with a prompt that specifically makes it adversarial to the original author. If possible, use a stronger model for the review than you did for the implementation: even though stronger models are more expensive, the review typically takes less time and its improved ability to find bugs will save you time and money in the long run.

Build a prompt by "discussing" what you want to accomplish with your LLM, even if you already know how you want it done. Capture your rules as instructions, the recurring themes as skills. Be clear with what you want, review the generated code and correct the instructions until the model gets it right, then use those same instructions, those same skills, for future work on the same codebase. Using instructions and skills keeps your context (relatively) small for longer working sessions. This is important because LLMs tend to "forget" their guardrails with longer-running sessions, so explicitly including programming paradigms, clear guidelines on code structure and design, rationale for design decisions and decisions on what you do not want to see in the instruction set allows you to reinforce those decisions without having to type them out every time, and allows you to refer the (adversarial) review agent to them.

Before you do that, though, decide how you are going to test what you want the LLM to write for you. Why are you asking it to write code? What do you want the code to be able to do? How will you demonstrate that it does what's intended? You should have a very good idea of what the solution looks like functionally before you ask the AI agent to start writing code for you. Otherwise, you won't know what to look for in those reviews, those tests, or the code.

Finally, do now allow the LLM to write code you do not understand. To effectively use an LLM you have to be better than the LLM. Learn what you don't know. Start by learning how to tests.
